What does Y tell you about X? Mutual information, the overlap between two uncertainties.

Mutual information measures how dependent two random variables are, in bits. Each section has three parts. Play lets you build joint distributions, scatter plots, noisy channels and coin flips and watch the information move. Math, slowly walks through the book's equations one step at a time. §6.3.1

Definition: how far is the joint from "independent"?

If X and Y were independent, their joint would just be the product of the marginals, \(p(x)p(y)\). Mutual information takes the real joint \(p(x,y)\) and asks how far it is from that independent version. It measures the distance with the KL divergence from §6.2. The bigger the gap, the more the two variables depend on each other.

Math, slowlyEquations 6.47 – 6.48

Equation 6.47, colored by role

\[ \mathbb{I}(X;Y) \;\triangleq\; D_{\mathbb{KL}}\big(\class{tx}{p(x,y)} \,\|\, \class{ta}{p(x)\,p(y)}\big) \;=\; \sum_{y\in\mathcal Y}\sum_{x\in\mathcal X} \class{tx}{p(x,y)}\,\log \class{tm}{\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}} \]
  • Weight \(p(x,y)\): how often the pair \((x,y)\) actually happens. Every cell is weighted by the truth.
  • Baseline \(p(x)p(y)\): how often that pair would happen if X and Y had nothing to do with each other. It's built only from the two marginals (the row and column sums).
  • The ratio: "how many times more often than chance does this pair occur?" Ratio 2 → \(\log_2 2 = +1\) bit. Ratio 1 → 0 (this cell looks independent). Ratio ½ → −1 bit (this pair is rarer than chance).
KL refresher (§6.2)
\(D_{\mathbb{KL}}(p\,\|\,q) = \sum_k p_k \log \frac{p_k}{q_k}\) is the number of extra bits you waste when the data comes from \(p\) but you planned for \(q\). It's always \(\ge 0\) (Gibbs' inequality), and it equals 0 only when \(p = q\). For MI, the "planned for" model is the independence model \(q = p(x)p(y)\).

Why MI is never negative (6.48)

  1. \(\mathbb{I}(X;Y) = D_{\mathbb{KL}}\big(p(x,y)\,\|\,p(x)p(y)\big)\)
    Definition. Both arguments are valid distributions over pairs: \(p(x)p(y)\) is non-negative and sums to \(\sum_x p(x)\sum_y p(y) = 1\).
  2. \(\ge 0\)
    Any KL divergence is non-negative. Individual cells can be negative (the amber ones), but those cells have small weight relative to the purple ones, so the total never dips below 0. The "negative cells sum" stat in Play shows how much they pull back.
  3. \(= 0 \iff p(x,y) = p(x)p(y)\ \text{for every } (x,y)\)
    KL is zero only when the two distributions match exactly, and that is the definition of independence. Try "Independent" or drag the blend slider to 1.

Worked example: a perfect copy

X is a fair coin and Y = X. The joint has \(\tfrac12\) on (0,0) and (1,1) and 0 elsewhere. Every product cell is \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14\).

\[ \mathbb I = \underbrace{\tfrac12\log_2\frac{1/2}{1/4}}_{(0,0)} + \underbrace{\tfrac12\log_2\frac{1/2}{1/4}}_{(1,1)} + \underbrace{0 + 0}_{\text{off-diagonal}} = \tfrac12(1) + \tfrac12(1) = 1 \text{ bit} \]

Zero-probability cells contribute nothing (using the convention \(0\log 0 = 0\)). Seeing Y gives you X's single bit for free. In the 3×3 Play grid, "Y = X exactly" gives \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.58\) bits for the same reason.

Notation. The book writes \(\mathbb I(X;Y)\) with a semicolon, not a comma, so it can say things like \(\mathbb I(X;Y,Z)\): the information between X and the pair (Y, Z). For continuous variables, swap the sums for integrals; the result is still \(\ge 0\).

Interpretation: the overlap of two uncertainties

MI can be rewritten using only entropies, and those rewrites fit in a two-circle diagram (Figure 6.4). Each circle is the uncertainty of one variable, the union is the joint entropy, and the overlap is the mutual information: the uncertainty you remove from one variable by observing the other.

Math, slowlyEquations 6.49 – 6.51

Deriving 6.49 from the definition

  1. \(\mathbb I(X;Y) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log\dfrac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}\)
    Start from Eq. 6.47.
  2. \(= \sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log\dfrac{p(x|y)}{p(x)}\)
    Conditional probability: \(p(x,y)/p(y) = p(x|y)\).
  3. \(= -\sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log p(x) \;+\; \sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log p(x|y)\)
    Log of a ratio = difference of logs. Split into two sums.
  4. \(= \class{tx}{-\sum_{x} p(x)\log p(x)} \;-\; \class{ta}{\Big(-\sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log p(x|y)\Big)}\)
    In the first sum, \(\log p(x)\) doesn't depend on y, so summing \(p(x,y)\) over y gives \(p(x)\). The second sum is rewritten with two minus signs so it matches the conditional entropy formula from §6.1.4.
  5. \(= \class{tx}{\mathbb H(X)} - \class{ta}{\mathbb H(X|Y)}\)
    Recognize each piece: marginal entropy minus conditional entropy. Swapping the roles of X and Y gives \(\mathbb H(Y) - \mathbb H(Y|X)\).

Reading: \(\mathbb H(X)\) is how unsure you are about X before looking at anything. \(\mathbb H(X|Y)\) is how unsure you still are, on average, after seeing Y. MI is the drop. And since \(\mathbb I \ge 0\), the drop can't be negative: \(\mathbb H(X|Y) \le \mathbb H(X)\). That's a second proof that conditioning reduces entropy on average.

Getting 6.51 and 6.50

  1. \(\mathbb H(X|Y) = \mathbb H(X,Y) - \mathbb H(Y)\)
    Chain rule for entropy (Eq. 6.15): uncertainty left in X after Y = uncertainty of the pair − uncertainty of Y.
  2. \(\mathbb I = \mathbb H(X) - \big[\mathbb H(X,Y) - \mathbb H(Y)\big] = \mathbb H(X) + \mathbb H(Y) - \mathbb H(X,Y)\)
    Substitute into 6.49. This is 6.51. In the diagram: adding both circles counts the overlap twice, and subtracting the union removes it once, leaving the overlap.
  3. \(\mathbb H(X|Y) + \mathbb H(Y|X) = 2\,\mathbb H(X,Y) - \mathbb H(X) - \mathbb H(Y) = \mathbb H(X,Y) - \mathbb I\)
    Apply the chain rule to both conditionals, then use 6.51 to replace \(\mathbb H(X)+\mathbb H(Y)-\mathbb H(X,Y)\) with \(\mathbb I\).
  4. \(\mathbb I = \mathbb H(X,Y) - \mathbb H(X|Y) - \mathbb H(Y|X)\)
    Rearrange: this is 6.50. The union minus the two crescents is the overlap. Equivalently \(\mathbb H(X,Y) = \mathbb H(X|Y) + \mathbb I + \mathbb H(Y|X)\), the middle bar in Play.

Example: is an even number likely to be prime?

Math, slowlyEquations 6.52 – 6.55

Step 1: the joint table and its rows

For n ∈ {1,…,8}: even & prime = {2}; even & not prime = {4, 6, 8}; odd & prime = {3, 5, 7}; odd & not prime = {1}. Dividing each row by its total (4 numbers each) gives \(p(Y|X)\):

Y=0 (not prime)Y=1 (prime)row sum
X=0 (odd)1/8 → 1/43/8 → 3/41/2
X=1 (even)3/8 → 3/41/8 → 1/41/2

Four of the eight numbers are even and four are prime, so each row and each column sums to 1/2. Both marginals are fair coins: \(\mathbb H(X) = \mathbb H(Y) = 1\) bit.

Step 2: conditional entropy (6.52)

\[ \mathbb H(Y|X) = -\sum_{x,y}\class{tx}{p(x,y)}\,\log_2 \class{ty}{p(y|x)} = -\Big[\tfrac18\log_2\tfrac14 + \tfrac38\log_2\tfrac34 + \tfrac38\log_2\tfrac34 + \tfrac18\log_2\tfrac14\Big] \]

Notice the mix: the weights are joint probabilities (how often each cell happens) but the logs use the conditional (how surprised you are by Y once you know X). With \(\log_2\tfrac14 = -2\) and \(\log_2\tfrac34 \approx -0.415\):

\[ \mathbb H(Y|X) = \underbrace{2\cdot\tfrac18\cdot 2}_{0.500} + \underbrace{2\cdot\tfrac38\cdot 0.415}_{0.311} \approx 0.81 \text{ bits} \]

Step 3: MI (6.53)

\[ \mathbb I(X;Y) = \mathbb H(Y) - \mathbb H(Y|X) = 1 - 0.81 = 0.19 \text{ bits} \]

Knowing parity shaves about a fifth of a bit off your uncertainty about primality. Not much, because each row is still a 1:3 / 3:1 split, not a certainty.

Step 4: check with the diagram (6.54–6.55)

The table is symmetric (swap X and Y and you get the same numbers), so \(\mathbb H(X|Y) = 0.81\) too.

\[ \mathbb H(X,Y) = \class{tx}{\mathbb H(X|Y)} + \class{tm}{\mathbb I(X;Y)} + \class{ty}{\mathbb H(Y|X)} = 0.81 + 0.19 + 0.81 = 1.81 \text{ bits} \]

This matches computing the joint entropy directly from \([\tfrac18,\tfrac38,\tfrac38,\tfrac18]\), as in §6.1.3. Move the N slider in Play: the identity holds for every N, even though the numbers change.

§6.3.4

Conditional MI: what's left once Z is known

\(\mathbb I(X;Y|Z)\) asks: after you already know a third variable Z, how much does Y still tell you about X? Conditioning on Z can remove dependence (Z explained it all), shrink it (Z already told you some of it), or even create dependence that wasn't there before.

Math, slowlyEquations 6.56 – 6.63

Definition as an average over Z (6.56–6.57)

\[ \mathbb I(X;Y|Z) \triangleq \class{tg}{\mathbb E_{p(Z)}}\big[\,\class{tm}{\mathbb I(X;Y)\mid Z}\,\big] = \sum_z \class{tg}{p(z)}\;\class{tm}{\sum_{x,y} p(x,y|z)\log\frac{p(x,y|z)}{p(x|z)\,p(y|z)}} \]
  • Inside: fix Z = z, take the slice \(p(x,y|z)\) of the table, and compute ordinary MI on that slice. That's the left and middle grids in Play.
  • Outside: average the slice MIs, weighted by how often each z happens. Eq. 6.57 folds both sums into a single expectation over \(p(x,y,z)\).

The entropy forms (6.58–6.60)

Every identity from §6.3.2 still holds; just add "\(|Z\)" to everything:

\[ \mathbb I(X;Y|Z) = \mathbb H(X|Z) + \mathbb H(Y|Z) - \mathbb H(X,Y|Z) = \mathbb H(X|Z) - \mathbb H(X|Y,Z) \]

Writing each conditional entropy as a difference of joint entropies (\(\mathbb H(A|Z) = \mathbb H(A,Z) - \mathbb H(Z)\)) gives 6.60, the form Play uses to compute the numbers:

\[ \mathbb I(X;Y|Z) = \mathbb H(X,Z) + \mathbb H(Y,Z) - \mathbb H(Z) - \mathbb H(X,Y,Z) \]

"Residual information" (6.61), derived

  1. \(\mathbb I(Y;X,Z) - \mathbb I(Y;Z)\)
    What (X, Z) together tell you about Y, minus what Z alone tells you.
  2. \(= \big[\mathbb H(Y) - \mathbb H(Y|X,Z)\big] - \big[\mathbb H(Y) - \mathbb H(Y|Z)\big]\)
    Apply 6.49 to each MI.
  3. \(= \mathbb H(Y|Z) - \mathbb H(Y|X,Z) = \mathbb I(X;Y|Z)\)
    The \(\mathbb H(Y)\) terms cancel; what remains is 6.59. So conditional MI is the extra information X adds about Y beyond what Z already gave.

Chain rule (6.62–6.63)

\[ \mathbb I(Z,Y;X) = \class{tx}{\mathbb I(Z;X)} + \class{tg}{\mathbb I(Y;X|Z)} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbb I(Z_1,\dots,Z_N;X) = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb I(Z_n;X\mid Z_1,\dots,Z_{n-1}) \]

Reveal the variables one at a time. Each one contributes only what it adds given everything revealed before it. The order changes the individual pieces but never the total (the two bars in Play).

Worked example: XOR

X, Y are independent fair coins and \(Z = X \oplus Y\) (1 when they differ). Alone, Y says nothing about X: \(\mathbb I(X;Y) = 0\). But once you know Z, Y tells you X exactly (\(X = Y \oplus Z\)):

\[ \mathbb I(X;Y|Z) = \mathbb H(X|Z) - \mathbb H(X|Y,Z) = 1 - 0 = 1 \text{ bit} \]
So conditioning can increase MI, unlike entropy, where conditioning can only decrease it on average. In a three-circle Venn diagram, XOR's center region would be \(\mathbb I(X;Y) - \mathbb I(X;Y|Z) = -1\) bit: the negative region warned about in §6.3.2.

§6.3.5

MI as a "generalized correlation coefficient"

For a jointly Gaussian pair, MI is a simple function of the correlation ρ: \(\mathbb I = -\tfrac12\log(1-\rho^2)\). Unlike ρ, though, MI also picks up non-linear relationships. A parabola or a circle can have ρ ≈ 0 and still have a lot of mutual information. The catch is that for real data you have to estimate MI, and the simplest estimator (binning) is sensitive to your choices.

Math, slowlyEquations 6.64 – 6.70

Setup (6.64)

\[ \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} \sim \mathcal N\!\left(\mathbf 0,\; \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}\sigma^2 & \rho\sigma^2\\ \rho\sigma^2 & \sigma^2\end{pmatrix}\right) \]

Both variables have variance \(\sigma^2\); the off-diagonal \(\rho\sigma^2\) is their covariance, so ρ is their correlation.

Tool from §6.1.6 (Eq. 6.26)
The differential entropy of a d-dimensional Gaussian is \(h = \tfrac12\log\big[(2\pi e)^d \det\Sigma\big]\). It depends only on the covariance (spread), never on the mean. For d = 1 this is \(\tfrac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)\).
  1. \(\det\Sigma = \sigma^2\cdot\sigma^2 - (\rho\sigma^2)^2 = \sigma^4(1-\rho^2)\)
    2×2 determinant: \(ad - bc\).
  2. \(h(X,Y) = \tfrac12\log\big[(2\pi e)^2\sigma^4(1-\rho^2)\big]\)
    Eq. 6.65: plug \(d=2\) and the determinant into the Gaussian entropy formula.
  3. \(h(X) = h(Y) = \tfrac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)\)
    Eq. 6.66: each marginal is a 1-D Gaussian with variance \(\sigma^2\).
  4. \(\mathbb I = h(X) + h(Y) - h(X,Y) = \log(2\pi e\sigma^2) - \tfrac12\log\big[(2\pi e)^2\sigma^4(1-\rho^2)\big]\)
    Eqs. 6.67–6.68: the 6.51 identity works for differential entropy too. The two marginal halves add to one full log.
  5. \(= \tfrac12\log\big[(2\pi e\sigma^2)^2\big] - \tfrac12\log\big[(2\pi e\sigma^2)^2(1-\rho^2)\big]\)
    Eq. 6.69: write \(\log a = \tfrac12\log a^2\) and regroup \((2\pi e)^2\sigma^4 = (2\pi e\sigma^2)^2\) so both terms share a factor.
  6. \(= \tfrac12\log\dfrac{1}{1-\rho^2} = \class{tm}{-\tfrac12\log(1-\rho^2)}\)
    Eq. 6.70: \(\log a - \log(ab) = -\log b\). Everything about \(\sigma\) and \(2\pi e\) cancels: MI ignores the units and scale of the variables.

  • ρ = 0: independent (for Gaussians, uncorrelated means independent), so \(\mathbb I = 0\).
  • ρ = ±1: Y = ±X. Seeing Y gives X's real value exactly, which is infinitely many bits of precision, so \(\mathbb I = \infty\).
  • Only \(\rho^2\) appears, so the sign doesn't matter: \(\rho = -0.8\) and \(+0.8\) carry the same information. You can invert it: \(\rho^2 = 1 - 2^{-2\mathbb I}\). That's why MI is called a generalized correlation.

When the data isn't Gaussian: estimating MI

The binning (histogram) estimator chops each axis into B bins, counts points per cell to get an empirical joint pmf, and plugs that into Eq. 6.47. The problem is that the answer depends on B and on where the bin edges fall. Too many bins for the sample size means most cells hold 0 or 1 point, the empirical table looks "structured" even for pure noise, and the MI estimate is biased upward. Set the shape to "Independent" and push the bins slider to 40 to see it. Adaptive estimators like KSG (k-nearest-neighbor distances, used by sklearn.feature_selection.mutual_info_regression) avoid choosing bins.

The book's logs here are natural logs (nats). This page shows bits; divide by \(\ln 2\) ≈ 0.693 to convert nats to bits.

§6.3.8

Data processing inequality: you can't un-lose information

X is something you care about, Y is a noisy observation of it, and Z is anything you compute from Y. If Z only "sees" X through Y (a Markov chain \(X \to Y \to Z\)), then Z can never know more about X than Y did. Processing can throw information away or keep it, but it can't create any.

Math, slowlyTheorem 6.3.1, Equations 6.77 – 6.79

What the Markov chain means

\(X \to Y \to Z\) means \(p(x,y,z) = p(x)\,p(y|x)\,p(z|y)\): Z is generated from Y alone. Equivalently \(X \perp Z \mid Y\): once you know Y, X tells you nothing more about Z. (The three-copies panel fits: Z is computed from Y.)

The proof, one line at a time

  1. \(\mathbb I(X;Y,Z) = \class{tx}{\mathbb I(X;Z)} + \class{ta}{\mathbb I(X;Y|Z)}\)
    Eq. 6.77: chain rule (6.62), revealing Z first then Y.
  2. \(\mathbb I(X;Y,Z) = \class{tm}{\mathbb I(X;Y)} + \class{tg}{\mathbb I(X;Z|Y)}\)
    Eq. 6.78: same chain rule, revealing Y first then Z. The total is the same either way.
  3. \(\class{tg}{\mathbb I(X;Z|Y)} = 0\)
    Markov property \(X\perp Z\mid Y\): within each slice Y = y, X and Z are independent, so every slice MI is 0, and so is their average.
  4. \(\class{tx}{\mathbb I(X;Z)} + \class{ta}{\mathbb I(X;Y|Z)} = \class{tm}{\mathbb I(X;Y)}\)
    Eq. 6.79: set the two expansions equal and drop the zero.
  5. \(\class{tm}{\mathbb I(X;Y)} \ge \class{tx}{\mathbb I(X;Z)}\)
    \(\mathbb I(X;Y|Z) \ge 0\), like any MI. The amount you lose is exactly \(\mathbb I(X;Y|Z)\): what Y still knew about X that Z threw away. Same argument gives \(\mathbb I(Y;Z)\ge\mathbb I(X;Z)\).

Why the telephone bars only go down

Each hop is a processing step: \(X \to Y_1 \to Y_2 \to \cdots\). Two hops flip a bit only if exactly one of them flips it, so the total flip probability after k hops is

\[ \varepsilon_k = \tfrac12\big(1 - (1-2\varepsilon)^k\big), \qquad \mathbb I(X;Y_k) = 1 - \mathbb H_b(\varepsilon_k) \text{ bits for a fair-coin X}, \]

where \(\mathbb H_b\) is the binary entropy from §6.1. As k grows, \(\varepsilon_k \to \tfrac12\) and the information goes to 0. No later hop can fix an earlier mistake, because it only sees the corrupted copy.

Information vs. accuracy. Majority vote gets the same best-guess accuracy as using all three copies, but it still loses information: it lumps "000" (very sure X = 0) with "001" (less sure). DPI is about information, which keeps track of how confident you can be, not just which answer you'd guess. "Count of 1s" loses nothing, because patterns with the same count have the same likelihood ratio. That idea is the next section.

§6.3.9

Sufficient statistics: compress without losing θ

Apply DPI to learning: an unknown parameter θ generates data D, and you summarize the data with a statistic s(D). The chain \(\theta \to D \to s(D)\) means the summary can't know more about θ than the raw data. If it knows exactly as much, the summary is sufficient: you can throw the raw data away and lose nothing about θ.

Math, slowlyEquations 6.80 – 6.81

DPI applied to statistics (6.80)

\[ \theta \to D \to s(D) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbb I\big(\theta; s(D)\big) \;\le\; \mathbb I(\theta; D) \]

s(D) is computed from D alone, so it's a Markov chain and DPI applies. Sufficient means equality: \(\mathbb I(\theta; s(D)) = \mathbb I(\theta; D)\). Then the chain can also be written \(\theta \to s(D) \to D\): given the summary, the rest of the data is just noise that doesn't depend on θ.

Why N₁ (the number of heads) is sufficient for a coin

  1. \(p(D|\theta) = \prod_{n=1}^{N}\theta^{x_n}(1-\theta)^{1-x_n}\)
    Flips are independent; each contributes θ if heads (\(x_n=1\)) and \(1-\theta\) if tails.
  2. \(= \theta^{\class{tm}{N_1}}(1-\theta)^{N-\class{tm}{N_1}}\)
    Collect the exponents: \(N_1 = \sum_n \mathbb I(x_n = 1)\). The order of the flips has disappeared, and only the count is left.
  3. \(p(\theta|D) \propto p(\theta)\,\theta^{N_1}(1-\theta)^{N-N_1} = p(\theta|N_1)\)
    Bayes' rule. Two datasets with the same N₁ give identical posteriors. Try "Shuffle the order": the blue curve doesn't move.
  4. \(p(D\,|\,N_1,\theta) = 1\big/\tbinom{N}{N_1}\)
    Given the count, every ordering is equally likely no matter what θ is. So once you know N₁, the sequence carries no further information about θ: \(\theta \to N_1 \to D\).

Minimal sufficient statistics (6.81)

\[ \theta \;\to\; \class{tm}{s(D)} \;\to\; s'(D) \;\to\; D \]

Many statistics are sufficient: D itself is (trivially), and so is "the sorted sequence" or "(N₁, the first flip)". A minimal sufficient statistic s keeps the relevant information and nothing else: it can be computed from any other sufficient statistic, \(s(D) = f(s'(D))\). Each step rightward in the chain adds redundant detail. For N coin flips, the minimal one is \((N, N_1)\).

Same idea, Gaussian mean
With known variance, \(p(D|\mu) \propto \exp\!\big(\tfrac{\mu}{\sigma^2}\sum_n x_n - \tfrac{N\mu^2}{2\sigma^2}\big)\) times terms without μ. The data enters only through \(\sum_n x_n\), equivalently the empirical mean \(\bar x\) and N. Those two numbers are all you need to infer μ.

MI (6.47)\(D_{\mathbb{KL}}(p(x,y)\,\|\,p(x)p(y))\)how far the joint is from independence; \(\ge 0\), \(=0\) iff independent
6.49\(\mathbb H(X) - \mathbb H(X|Y)\)uncertainty about X removed by seeing Y (symmetric)
6.51\(\mathbb H(X) + \mathbb H(Y) - \mathbb H(X,Y)\)overlap of the two circles
Bounds\(0 \le \mathbb I(X;Y) \le \min(\mathbb H(X), \mathbb H(Y))\)can't share more than either one has
Conditional MI\(\mathbb H(X|Z) - \mathbb H(X|Y,Z)\)what Y adds about X beyond Z; can be larger or smaller than \(\mathbb I(X;Y)\)
Chain rule\(\mathbb I(Z,Y;X) = \mathbb I(Z;X) + \mathbb I(Y;X|Z)\)reveal variables one at a time
Gaussian\(-\tfrac12\log(1-\rho^2)\)0 at ρ = 0, ∞ at ρ = ±1, depends only on ρ²
DPI\(X\to Y\to Z \Rightarrow \mathbb I(X;Y)\ge \mathbb I(X;Z)\)processing can't create information
Sufficient s(D)\(\mathbb I(\theta;s(D)) = \mathbb I(\theta;D)\)a summary that loses nothing about θ (coins: N₁)
Built as a study companion for CS 473 from Murphy, Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction, §6.3 (§6.3.6, 6.3.7 and 6.3.10 omitted).
